Image Compressor: A Practical Guide

Image compression can make photos easier to upload, share, and publish. The right balance depends on how the image will be used: a small thumbnail needs less detail than a large product image or printed photograph.

When to use Image Compressor

  • Reduce photos before attaching them to email.
  • Prepare images for website pages and online forms.
  • Shrink images that exceed upload size limits.

How to use the Image Compressor tool

  1. Open Image Compressor and choose a supported image file.
  2. Select the available compression or quality setting if offered.
  3. Process the image and download the compressed version.
  4. Compare the new file with the original at normal viewing size and check that text or fine detail remains clear.

Tips for a better result

  • Keep the original for future high-quality exports.
  • Resize dimensions as well as compressing when the image will only appear small.
  • Use a suitable format: JPEG is common for photos, while PNG can be better for sharp graphics.

Understanding lossy compression

Image compression reduces file size by removing detail that is hard to notice. Quality settings control how much is removed. At high quality the changes are invisible; at low quality you see blocky areas, smeared edges and colour banding. Photographs tolerate compression well because they contain noise and gradients, while screenshots, logos and text-heavy images show artefacts sooner.

Picking a size target

Think about where the image will be used. Email attachments and form uploads often have size limits, such as 1 MB or 2 MB. Web images look best when sized close to the dimensions they will be displayed at, so a 4000-pixel-wide photo shown in a 800-pixel slot wastes bandwidth. Reduce dimensions first if you can, then adjust quality in small steps and compare the output with the original.

Workflow tips

Always keep the original file. Compress a copy, zoom in at 100 percent to check faces, text and fine textures, and stop at the smallest size that still looks right. Avoid compressing the same file repeatedly, since each pass loses more detail. If the image will be edited later, store a high-quality master and make compressed copies only for sharing.

More questions answered

Which format compresses best?

For photos, JPG or WebP give small files. For simple graphics, PNG keeps sharp edges but may be larger.

Will the image get blurry?

Heavy compression can soften detail. Compare results at different quality settings and pick the lowest one that still looks good.

Are my images uploaded to a server?

Supported processing happens in your browser, so your images are not uploaded to PDF Engine.

Limitations and privacy

Results depend on the input file, browser memory, and the supported features of the PDF or image format. Encrypted, damaged, unusually large, or complex files may fail. PDF Engine is designed for supported processing in your browser, but the page may load third-party JavaScript libraries. Keep the original file and check the output before sharing. For sensitive documents, follow your organization's data-handling rules.
